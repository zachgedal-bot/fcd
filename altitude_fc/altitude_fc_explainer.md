# Altitude FC — what the research says, and what the cards show

Playing soccer high above sea level hurts a visiting team's engine, not its legs. The best-controlled soccer studies show that the amount of hard running a team can produce falls within hours of arriving at altitude, recovers only partly over the following two weeks, and never fully returns to sea-level values. Single sprints are, if anything, marginally faster because the air is thinner. There is no good evidence that passing or shooting technique gets worse — but the ball itself flies differently, which is a physics problem that affects both teams equally. Teams that live at altitude avoid the acute penalty at home, yet they are not "super-charged": their own aerobic ceiling is still lower than it would be at sea level, and match data do not show them gaining anything when they descend.

## 1. The strongest findings, in everyday language

**The engine shrinks with height.** In trained endurance athletes, the body's maximum oxygen delivery (VO2max) falls in a straight line by about 6% for every 1,000 m of altitude, and the drop is already measurable at 800 m ([Wehrlin & Hallén 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-005-0081-9)). At 3,600 m (La Paz) reviews put the acute loss at roughly 20–25% ([Gore et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2008.00836.x)). Soccer is an interval sport that runs on this engine, so the ceiling on repeatable hard running comes down with it.

**Match running falls, even at "low" altitude.** Elite youth internationals playing at 1,600 m four days after arrival covered about 9% less total distance and 15% less high-velocity running than in sea-level matches; the total-distance deficit was still detectable on day 6 ([Garvican et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2012-0375); percentages as summarised in [Billaut & Aughey 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092834)). At the 2010 World Cup, professional teams covered 3.1% less total distance at 1,200–1,750 m than at sea level, with top speed, goals and goalkeeper errors unchanged ([Nassis 2013](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e31825d999d)). The pro number is smaller than the youth number, partly because World Cup teams had spent time on site and partly because the youth study measured high-velocity running specifically.

**At 3,600 m, two weeks is not enough.** In the ISA3600 project — an Australian U17 team and a Bolivian U20 team playing five matches at 430 m and 3,600 m — the Australians arrived with moderate-to-large impairments in wellbeing and Yo-Yo intermittent-recovery performance, acclimatised much more slowly than the Bolivians, and had not regained their near-sea-level high-intensity running after 13 days ([Buchheit et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092749); [Aughey et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092776)). Their arterial oxygen content crashed within two hours of landing, then recovered over days mainly because the blood thickened (plasma volume fell ~400 mL); haemoglobin mass rose only 3% in 12 days ([Wachsmuth et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092761)). The authors explicitly argue against "fly in, play, fly out".

**Altitude residents are helped, not immune.** The Bolivian players carried about 10% more haemoglobin per kilogram than the Australians and slept normally at 3,600 m, while the Australians slept worse for at least two weeks ([Roach et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092843)). Yet the Bolivians' match running also dropped at 3,600 m relative to their own near-sea-level games, and the ISA3600 authors concluded that neither 13 days of acclimatisation nor lifelong residence fully protects match activity ([Aughey et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092776)). Kenyan runners living at ~2,000 m have a VO2max roughly 15% lower at home than at sea level ([Saltin et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.1995.tb00037.x)); high-altitude natives show a smaller decrement with hypoxia, but not a zero one ([Brutsaert 2008](https://doi.org/10.1139/h08-009)).

**Repeated sprints suffer; single sprints do not.** In team-sport athletes doing sets of short sprints in simulated altitude, the first set was intact until 4,000 m but later sets lost power at every altitude from 2,000 m up ([Goods et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2013-0423)). Over eight 5-s sprints, mean distance fell about 2.6% at a 3,600 m-equivalent and did not change at a ~1,900 m-equivalent ([Townsend et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1113/ep088485)); classic chamber work showed the same pattern with higher lactate ([Balsom et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-1716.1994.tb09807.x)). The mechanism is slower recovery of muscle energy stores between efforts when oxygen is scarce ([Levine, Stray-Gundersen & Mehta 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2008.00835.x)). Meanwhile thinner air lowers drag: Olympic 100 m times at Mexico City (2,250 m) were about 0.19 s faster ([Linthorne 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports4020029)). For 10–30 m soccer sprints the benefit is small, and the ISA3600 squads' 20 m sprint actually got slightly worse over the camp, probably from fatigue.

**Technique and decisions: no soccer evidence of a decline.** The only soccer study of skill at altitude found pass completion *rose* by about 5.6 percentage points above 2,500 m, driven by safer passes in the team's own half — a change in decisions, not in ability ([Tovar 2014](https://doi.org/10.1515/jqas-2014-0035)). Lab studies show acute hypoxia impairs cognition with a moderate effect size (Hedges g ≈ −0.49), but mainly when arterial oxygen is very low, i.e. at altitudes above the range soccer is played ([McMorris et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2017.01.019)). The cards therefore show **"insufficient evidence"** for SHO, PAS and DRI rather than a number.

**Match results overstate the physiology.** Across 1,460 South American internationals, each 1,000 m of altitude difference was worth about half a goal to the higher team, and the home-win probability rose from 0.54 to 0.83 for a 3,700 m gap ([McSharry 2007](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.39393.451516.ad)). But once team quality is controlled, the altitude effect on World Cup qualifiers is not statistically significant ([Chumacero 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/1527002509336217)), and Libertadores data suggest altitude clubs suffer *more* when they descend than lowland clubs do when they climb ([Cabrera Hernández et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.14198/jhse.2023.181.04)). Results mix altitude with home advantage, travel, crowds and tactics; they cannot isolate the physiology.

**Everyone is different.** Individual VO2max slopes range from 4.6 to 7.5% per 1,000 m around the 6.3% mean; half of one squad developed disordered breathing in sleep at 3,600 m while the other half did not ([Sargent et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092829)); and haemoglobin-mass "responders" one year were not reliably responders the next ([McLean et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092744)). Squad averages hide a wide spread.

## 2. Three kinds of statement, kept apart

| Layer | What it is | Examples in this project |
|---|---|---|
| **Measured finding** | A number someone measured | −6.3% VO2max per 1,000 m; −3.1% total distance at 1,200–1,750 m; Hb mass +3% in 12 d at 3,600 m |
| **Physiological explanation** | Why the number happens | less oxygen → lower aerobic ceiling → slower recovery between efforts; plasma-volume loss then slow red-cell gain |
| **Modelling assumption** | A choice we made to fill a gap | high-intensity running loses 0.7× the VO2max deficit; 40% of the acute deficit recovers with a 3.5-day time constant, 25% more with a 21-day constant, 35% never; rating sensitivity 0.4 points per 1% |

Everything in the third row is a slider in the interactive cards.

## 3. How to read the cards

Both hypothetical teams start at 80 in every category, meaning "equal ability, each measured in its own usual environment". The altitude-adjusted column shows how far each team is expected to move from *its own* baseline in the chosen match. So:

- A sea-level team at 3,600 m on day 1 shows the largest declines (model: about −13% high-intensity running, −5.5% repeated-sprint recovery, +1% top speed → STAMINA 74, REPEATED EFFORT 78, PAC 79 at the default sensitivity).
- The same team after 14 days shows a *smaller decline* (about −7% high-intensity running → STAMINA 77), not a recovery to 80.
- An altitude team at its home altitude shows 80 across the board: no acute penalty, but also no bonus — it is not "improving above baseline", it is simply not being hurt.
- An altitude team descending to sea level shows 80 with an explicit "insufficient evidence" flag on any bonus.

A 10% physiological change is **not** a 10-point rating change. With the default sensitivity of 0.4 it is 4 points; the slider goes from 0.1 to 1.0 so you can see how arbitrary that conversion is. Ratings are never summed into an overall score.

## 4. Ball flight is a separate story

Drag and the curving (Magnus) force on a ball are proportional to air density ([Goff 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12283-013-0117-z)). At 3,600 m the air is about 30% less dense, so a hard, flat 25 m/s kick carries about 8% further and a spinning ball bends roughly 30% less for the same spin. This is identical for both teams and does not acclimatise; the advantage goes to whichever team has practised with it. It is deliberately not folded into any player rating.

## 4b. Case study: Toluca, and the FIFA ban of 2007–08

**Toluca.** Estadio Nemesio Díez sits at about 2,670 m, the highest venue in Liga MX and roughly 430 m above Mexico City's Estadio Azteca (~2,240 m). By the FIFA zones it is "moderate altitude" (2,000–3,000 m). Applied to hypothetical teams with equal ability, the model gives:

| Scenario (teams hypothetical, venue altitudes real) | VO2max | High-intensity running | Repeated-sprint recovery | STAMINA (of 80) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coastal club (~10 m) → Toluca, arrives day before | −13.3% | −9.3% | −4.0% | 76 |
| Monterrey club (~540 m) → Toluca, day before | −11.9% | −8.4% | −3.6% | 76 |
| Monterrey club → Toluca, 3 days ahead (FIFA "recommended" tier) | −9.9% | −6.9% | −3.0% | 77 |
| Monterrey club → Toluca, 7 days ahead | −7.8% | −5.5% | −2.4% | 77 |
| Mexico City club (~2,240 m) → Toluca, day before | −2.4% | −1.7% | −0.7% | 79 |
| Toluca club → Monterrey or Mexico City | 0 (bonus: insufficient evidence) | 0 | 0 | 80 |

The ball at Toluca flies through air about 23% less dense than at sea level; a flat 25 m/s kick carries about 6% further and bends roughly a quarter less for the same spin.

**A warning from the one Liga MX study.** The only published tracking study of a Mexican club found *no* running penalty at moderate altitude: over 130 league matches (2014–2018) per-player distance was 9,855 m near sea level, 10,102 m at low altitude and 9,878 m at moderate altitude, with sprint counts and mean speed equally flat ([Alanis et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apunsm.2022.100391)). That directly contradicts the "coastal club" row above if taken at face value. The likely reasons are the reasons the model exists: Liga MX clubs play at 1,500–2,700 m every few weeks, many of their players have lived at altitude, and players who were substituted (often the most fatigued) were excluded. In model terms, a Mexican club is closer to the "Mexico City → Toluca" row than to the "coastal" row, and the study is evidence that habitual exposure matters — not that altitude is harmless. It is also a single club with camera-based data and no arrival-time control, so it cannot resolve the question on its own. Toluca's strong home record is real, but as with McSharry's South American data, wins mix altitude with squad quality, crowd and travel and cannot be attributed to physiology alone.

**FIFA's ban.** In May 2007, after a Brazilian federation complaint, FIFA's executive committee banned international matches above 2,500 m on grounds of player health and unfair home advantage — a line that caught La Paz (3,600 m), Quito (~2,800 m), Bogotá (~2,650 m), Cusco, and venues in Peru, Chile and Mexico, including Toluca. A month later, under CONMEBOL pressure, the limit was raised to 3,000 m so that Bogotá and Quito could host qualifiers while La Paz and Cusco could not; La Paz then received a provisional exemption. In December 2007 (Tokyo) FIFA replaced the ban with tiers — above 2,500 m three days' acclimatisation strongly recommended, above 2,750 m one week mandatory, above 3,000 m two weeks or no match — and upheld them in March 2008. In late May 2008 the whole ruling was suspended after a unanimous CONMEBOL protest (Brazil dissenting), and in 2010 FIFA said altitude was no longer on its agenda. Those three tiers are exactly the 3 / 7 / 14-day settings on the "days since arrival" slider; the cards now show which tier a chosen venue would have fallen into. The tiers came from the 2008 FIFA consensus (E14) plus negotiation, not from a measured performance cut-off — the model shows a continuous decline with no step at 2,500 or 2,750 m.

## 5. Where the model is weakest

1. The three soccer running anchors are one professional observational dataset (~1,500 m), one youth field study (1,600 m) and one youth expedition (3,600 m). There is no professional GPS study at 2,500–3,600 m with a controlled arrival schedule.
2. Repeated-sprint magnitudes come almost entirely from simulated (normobaric) hypoxia in short lab protocols; matches involve hundreds of efforts over 90 minutes.
3. The acclimatisation curve is fitted to cyclists at 2,340 m and youth footballers at 3,600 m; its shape at 1,500 m is extrapolated.
4. "Usual training altitude" is treated as full adaptation. Real squads mix residents and newcomers.
5. Technical execution and decision-making are unquantified by design; ball-flight familiarity is unquantified too.

## 6. Data that would improve the model

- **Match GPS / optical tracking** (total, high-intensity and sprint distance, accelerations) from professional matches at 0, 1,500, 2,500 and 3,600 m with **arrival dates** for the away team — the single most valuable dataset.
- **Team travel logs** (arrival time, pre-acclimatisation camps, simulated-altitude use) for CONMEBOL, Liga MX and Bolivian league fixtures.
- **Player-level residency histories** (where each player has lived/trained in the past 6–12 months), to replace the team-level "usual altitude".
- **Physiological markers**: haemoglobin mass, resting SpO2, sleep quality, wellness scores across the first 14 days at altitude for the same players.
- **Repeated-sprint tests in terrestrial altitude** for senior professionals, at 1,500 and 2,500 m as well as 3,600 m.
- **Event data** (pass length, pass success, shot speed, long-ball frequency) at matched altitudes to test technique and decision changes properly; **ball-tracking speeds** to check the physics.
- **Weather** (temperature, humidity, wind) for the same matches, so heat is not mistaken for altitude.
- **Match outcomes with team-strength ratings** (Elo or betting odds) to estimate the results effect net of quality and home advantage.
